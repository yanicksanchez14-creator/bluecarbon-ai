How it works

From a satellite image to a carbon estimate

Coastal wetlands store carbon in their soils for centuries, and protecting them can earn carbon credits. The first question for any project is what a site actually holds. Answering it used to take months of fieldwork.

1

A clear view from orbit

Every Sentinel-2 image of the site over a year is cleaned of cloud and combined into one clear picture at 10 m detail, plus a clear-water image that shows the seafloor.

2

Every patch of coast labelled

A deep-learning model trained on 39 coastal sites around the world labels each 10 by 10 m patch as mangrove, salt marsh, seagrass, tidal flat, water or land.

3

Carbon, with an honest range

Areas are corrected for the model's known errors, then multiplied by carbon measured in nearby soil cores, with IPCC values where none exist. Every number comes with a low and high estimate.

Accuracy

Tested on coastlines it has never seen

Scores come from whole estuaries and 5 km blocks kept out of training, compared against independent reference maps. We publish the weak spots too.

Mangrove and salt marsh are mapped reliably, including on estuaries the model never saw during training.

Seagrass is the hardest habitat to see from space, because it grows underwater and murky or deep water hides it. The model is cautious: it rarely calls open water seagrass, but misses some meadows. Treat seagrass areas as a lower bound.

Score shown is intersection over union (IoU): the overlap between the AI's map and the reference map, from 0 to 1.

Site screening

Is this coastline worth a carbon project?

A site screen answers that before anyone pays for fieldwork. It combines the habitat map with measured soil carbon, recent habitat change and the legal status of the area, and ends with a plain verdict.

Sample: Laguna de Términos, Mexico

  1. Habitat areasMangrove, salt marsh and seagrass in hectares, corrected for the model's known errors, with a likely range.
  2. Carbon stored and absorbedSoil and plant carbon from measured soil cores near the site where they exist, IPCC values where they don't.
  3. Credit potentialLow, mid and high credits per year after risk buffer, leakage and uncertainty deductions, and their value at current prices.
  4. Change over timeHabitat gained or lost in recent years, which decides whether a protection project can claim avoided loss.
  5. Rights and statusProtected areas, Ramsar listing, land tenure, carbon rights law and existing carbon projects, each with its source.
  6. VerdictPromising, needs investigation, or unlikely to qualify, with what to confirm on the ground first.

About

Built on open data, open to inspection

Every input is free and public: Copernicus Sentinel-2 imagery through Google Earth Engine, ESA WorldCover, global tidal flat and wetland maps, the Allen Coral Atlas, state and national seagrass surveys, and the Smithsonian Coastal Carbon Library.

The code is open source under the MIT License, so every number on this site can be traced back to its source and checked. BlueCarbon-AI is built by Yanick Sanchez and is in early development: site screens on request will open once the service is set up.
